| **Longitude wrap at the antimeridian**                                    | Longitude is cyclic: +180° and -180° are the same meridian. Any code that treats longitude as a plain number on a line — averaging it, interpolating it, building a bounding box from it, or summing differences along a track — produces a wrong answer for a flight that crosses the Pacific.                                                                                                                            | ADS-B does not transmit signed degrees at all. Airborne position messages carry Compact Position Reporting (CPR): 17-bit LAT-CPR (bits 55-71) and 17-bit LON-CPR (bits 72-88), each a fraction of a zone of 2^17 = 131072 steps, plus a 1-bit CPR format flag (0 = even frame, 1 = odd frame). A receiver needs one even and one odd frame to resolve an unambiguous global position; the scheme targets roughly 5 m airborne accuracy. Degrees only exist after decoding. | An Anchorage-Tokyo track passing the antimeridian near 51°N: consecutive reports read longitude 179.87°E then -179.94° (i.e. 179.94°W). The true leg is about 5.6 nm; naive subtraction gives a delta of -359.81°, roughly 13,600 nm at that latitude. (Reasoned worked example on a real route, not a published figure. The two longitudes and the great-circle arithmetic are the illustration; no source claims these specific reports.) | A track that crosses ±180°. Distance-along-track sums a spurious ~360° jump, a min/max bounding box spans the whole globe instead of a few miles, and a linear interpolation between the two points draws the aircraft backwards across every other continent. (Well-known class of geospatial defect; described here from the CPR/great-circle mechanics rather than a single citation.) | The obvious implementation treats latitude and longitude as independent scalars and uses ordinary arithmetic: delta = b.lon - a.lon, box = [min(lon), max(lon)]. Both assume longitude is monotonic and unbounded. It is neither — it wraps. Haversine happens to survive this because it takes the sine of the half-difference, so the ±360° is absorbed; almost nothing else does.                | Never subtract longitudes directly. Normalise any delta into (-180, 180] before use; compute distance with a great-circle formula rather than component differences; split a track into segments at the crossing before building a bounding box or drawing it; and for bounding boxes, allow west > east as a legitimate antimeridian-spanning box rather than swapping the values. (Standard geospatial practice, stated from the mechanics.) | RTCA DO-260B / ICAO Annex 10 Vol IV — Mode S extended squitter airborne position message, CPR encoding (the ICAO Annex 10 CPR decoding algorithm is reproduced in the linked NASA formal-verification paper, §2). | Alert on any single-report position delta implying a groundspeed above ~1200 kt, and on any computed leg longer than the aircraft's remaining range. A track-distance metric that jumps by a multiple of the earth's circumference is the signature. | ✅  |
| **High-latitude flight — true vs magnetic heading, meridian convergence** | Above roughly 70° latitude, magnetic heading becomes unusable — the magnetic poles are nearby, variation changes rapidly over short distances, and meridians converge so quickly that a constant true heading no longer means a constant direction of travel. Aircraft operating there switch to true or grid heading.                                                                                                     | ARINC 429 carries heading as separate labels with separate meanings: magnetic heading and true heading are distinct labels, each a BNR word with its own defined units, resolution and range. A consumer that reads 'the heading label' without checking which label it came from is reading an undefined quantity.                                                                                                                                                        | At Resolute Bay (CYRB, 74°43'N), magnetic variation is on the order of tens of degrees and unstable; charts and procedures in the Northern Domestic Airspace are referenced to true north rather than magnetic. (Canadian Northern Domestic Airspace is true-referenced; cited here from operating practice rather than a fetched source. Verify against the Transport Canada AIM before quoting a number.)                                 | A display or a derived track angle that assumes one heading reference. Mixing a magnetic heading from one aircraft with a true heading from another puts two aircraft on visibly different tracks while both are flying the same route, and a heading-vector glyph points somewhere the aircraft is not going. (Stated from the mechanism; no single citation.)                           | A schema with one `heading_deg` field cannot express which north it is referenced to, so the information is lost at ingest and can never be recovered downstream. The value looks perfectly valid — it is a number between 0 and 360 — which is why this survives review.                                                                                                                           | Carry the reference alongside the value (`heading_deg` + `heading_ref: magnetic/true/grid`), and convert only at the display boundary using a magnetic model for the aircraft's position and date. Treat track angle and heading as different quantities: heading is where the nose points, track is where the aircraft is going, and in wind they differ. (verify)                                                                            | ARINC 429 Part 1 — label assignments fix units, resolution, range and transmit interval per label; heading labels are distinct for magnetic and true.                                                             | Compare reported heading against the bearing derived from consecutive positions. A persistent offset that tracks local magnetic variation means the two references are being mixed.                                                                  | ✅  |
| **Position integrity categories (NIC / NACp / SIL)**                      | ADS-B does not just report a position; it reports how much that position should be trusted. NIC (Navigation Integrity Category) gives a containment radius around the reported position. NACp (Navigation Accuracy Category — Position) gives the estimated accuracy. SIL (Source Integrity Level) gives the probability that the true position lies outside the NIC containment radius, expressed per sample or per hour. | NIC, NACp, NACv, SIL and SDA are transmitted as small integer categories, not as metres. The defined values and their meanings are tabulated in RTCA DO-260B §2.2.3. Each integer maps to a containment radius or accuracy bound; the number itself carries no units.                                                                                                                                                                                                      | A compliant ADS-B Out installation under the US rule must meet NACp ≥ 8 and SIL = 3; a receiver seeing NACp drop to 0 on an aircraft that was previously reporting 9 is watching that aircraft's navigation source degrade in real time. (The 14 CFR 91.227 performance requirements are in the linked Federal Register rule; the worked degradation scenario is an illustration.)                                                          | GNSS jamming or spoofing. The aircraft keeps transmitting a position — the message is well-formed and the latitude/longitude parse cleanly — but NACp collapses and the position may be tens of miles wrong. A system that stores lat/lon and discards the integrity categories has no way to tell a good fix from a bad one, and will happily plot, alert on and store the bad one.      | The naive schema models position as latitude + longitude, which are the two fields a developer expects. The integrity categories arrive in the same message and are dropped because they have no column. From that point the system treats every position as equally true, so a degraded fix silently contaminates track history, distance totals and any geofence or proximity logic built on top. | Persist NIC, NACp and SIL with every position and expose them; refuse to use a position below a configured NACp for anything safety-adjacent; and show degradation in the UI rather than hiding it, because a sudden fleet-wide NACp drop is itself the signal that something is interfering with GNSS in a region.                                                                                                                            | RTCA DO-260B §2.2.3 (NIC/NACp/NACv/SIL/SDA value tables); 14 CFR 91.227 ADS-B Out performance requirements; EASA CS-ACNS for the European equivalent.                                                             | Chart NACp distribution across the fleet over time. A regional cluster of low-NACp aircraft is a GNSS interference event, not a fleet of broken transponders — and it is visible only if the field was stored.                                       | ✅  |
| **GNSS outage — coasting, dead reckoning and stale position**             | When the navigation source loses its fix, the aircraft does not stop transmitting. Depending on the installation it may transmit the last known position, transmit a dead-reckoned estimate, or transmit nothing while other fields keep flowing. All three look different on the wire and all three look identical to a naive consumer: a position arrived.                                                               | The integrity categories are the mechanism that expresses this: NIC degrades toward 0 as the containment radius grows, and SIL/SDA express how much confidence the source itself claims. DO-260B defines these values; there is no separate boolean 'this position is stale' field in the airborne position message.                                                                                                                                                       | An aircraft in an area of GNSS interference continues to report a position that does not change between successive messages while its altitude and airspeed continue to vary normally — a frozen fix on a still-flying aircraft. (Illustrative of the coasting signature; not a specific published incident.)                                                                                                                               | A repeated identical position. Track distance stops accumulating, computed groundspeed from position deltas reads zero while the reported groundspeed reads 300 kt, and any 'aircraft has landed' or 'aircraft is stationary' heuristic fires on an aircraft at cruise. (verify)                                                                                                          | The obvious implementation infers movement from position deltas and treats 'no movement' as a fact about the aircraft rather than a fact about the navigation source. It has no way to distinguish 'this aircraft is parked' from 'this aircraft's GPS is out' because both produce the same sequence of identical coordinates.                                                                     | Cross-check derived groundspeed against reported groundspeed and flag the disagreement rather than trusting either. Treat an unchanged position with changing altitude or airspeed as a data-quality event, not a movement event. Store the integrity categories so the real cause is visible. (verify)                                                                                                                                        | RTCA DO-260B — NIC/SIL semantics; the ADS-B Out performance rule defines the minimum acceptable categories, and by implication what degradation looks like.                                                       | A counter of reports whose position is byte-identical to the previous report for the same aircraft, split by whether other fields changed. Non-zero with changing altitude means coasting.                                                           | ✅  |

| **Barometric vs geometric altitude, QNH and the transition altitude** | There is no single 'altitude'. Barometric altitude is what the altimeter shows, derived from static pressure against a reference setting. Geometric altitude is a GNSS height above the WGS-84 ellipsoid. They are different quantities measuring different things, and they disagree by an amount that changes with the weather. | ADS-B transmits both, in different message type codes: type codes 9-18 carry barometric altitude, type codes 20-22 carry GNSS height above ellipsoid (HAE). Barometric altitude is encoded in 25 ft increments (with a 100 ft mode also defined). They are separate fields in separate messages, not two views of one number. | A published study of ADS-B messages found the absolute difference between geometric height and barometric altitude ranged from 25 ft to 1,325 ft, averaging 569 ft.                                                                                                     | Comparing an aircraft's altitude to terrain, to an airport elevation, or to another aircraft, using whichever altitude happened to arrive. Two aircraft at the same indicated flight level are vertically separated correctly; the same two compared by GNSS height may appear hundreds of feet apart. Conversely a barometric altitude compared against a terrain database — which is geometric — can be wrong by over a thousand feet. | A single `altitude_ft` column forces a choice at ingest that the ingest layer is not qualified to make, and erases the evidence of which was chosen. Every downstream consumer then assumes it is the one they need. Vertical separation wants barometric; terrain proximity wants geometric; the schema offers one field and no way to ask. | Store both, in separate nullable columns, and never synthesise one from the other. Above the transition altitude use pressure altitude referenced to the 1013.25 hPa standard setting (a flight level); below it use altitude referenced to local QNH. Compare like with like, and label which is displayed. | RTCA DO-260B — extended squitter type codes 9-18 (barometric) and 20-22 (GNSS HAE); ICAO Annex 10 Vol IV for the Mode S altitude encoding. | Track the running difference between the two altitudes per aircraft. It should vary slowly with weather; a step change means the reference setting changed, and a difference outside roughly ±1,500 ft means one of the two is wrong. | ✅  |
| **Vertical rate — barometric vs geometric source, and its noise**     | Vertical rate — climb or descent in feet per minute — can be derived from barometric altitude or from geometric height, and ADS-B says which. The two disagree, and both are noisier than the smooth number shown on a cockpit instrument.                                                                                        | The ADS-B airborne velocity message (type code 19) carries vertical rate together with a source bit distinguishing barometric from geometric, plus a sign bit and a magnitude field; the message also carries a separate difference between barometric and GNSS altitude.                                                     | A turboprop levelling off reports vertical rates oscillating between roughly +200 and -200 fpm for several reports while the aircraft is, operationally, in level flight. (Illustrative of quantisation and turbulence noise around zero, not a published measurement.) | Deriving flight phase from a single vertical-rate sample. Noise around zero at level-off flips the derived phase between climb, cruise and descent on consecutive reports, producing a burst of phase-change events for an aircraft that never changed what it was doing. (verify)                                                                                                                                                       | A threshold test on one sample — `vertical_rate > 300 ? 'climb' : ...` — has no hysteresis and no memory. Any signal that dwells near a threshold will chatter across it, and vertical rate dwells near zero for most of a flight.                                                                                                           | Require a state to persist for several consecutive reports before accepting a transition, use separate enter and exit thresholds (hysteresis), and combine vertical rate with altitude trend rather than relying on either alone. (verify)                                                                   | RTCA DO-260B — airborne velocity message, type code 19, including the vertical-rate source subfield.                                       | Count derived phase transitions per aircraft per hour. A healthy flight has a handful; chattering produces dozens and is immediately visible as a rate.                                                                               | ✅  |

| **ICAO 24-bit address vs registration vs callsign vs flight number**            | An aircraft has at least four identifiers and none of them is a primary key on its own. The ICAO 24-bit address is assigned by the registering state and burned into the transponder. The registration (tail number) is painted on the airframe. The callsign is what ATC says on the radio. The flight number is a commercial schedule label. They change on different timescales for different reasons. | The ICAO 24-bit address is exactly 24 bits, written as six hexadecimal characters (for example A9D286, 400936, 3C6745), globally unique, and allocated to states in blocks. Exactly one address is assigned per aircraft, and states are directed to take special care when releasing an address for reassignment. | A Canadian-registered aircraft carries a C- prefixed registration such as C-GABC and a separate hexadecimal ICAO address in the Canadian allocation block; the two are related only through the registry, and the callsign it flies under may be neither. (The C- prefix and the address/registration/callsign separation are general; a specific paired example needs a registry lookup.) | Keying fleet state on the registration. When an aircraft is re-registered — sold across borders, or moved between operators — the registration changes and, because the two are related by the registering state's assignment scheme, the ICAO address is normally reassigned to match the new registry. Keyed on registration, the system now has two aircraft where there is one, and the history splits. | The obvious model picks the identifier a human would use — the tail number — and makes it the primary key, because it is the one that appears in conversation and on the aircraft. It is the least stable of the four. Worse, transponders are reprogrammable and are occasionally moved between airframes, so even the ICAO address is not a permanent airframe identity. | Use an internal surrogate key for the airframe, and treat the ICAO address, registration and callsign as time-bounded attributes of it, each with a validity interval. Verification of the configured address is expected after a major maintenance check or a change of registry, which is exactly when the mapping moves. | ICAO Annex 10 Vol III — 24-bit aircraft address allocation; ICAO APIRG guidance on assignment of 24-bit addresses and II codes for SSR Mode S. | Alert when a known ICAO address starts reporting a different registration, or when a registration appears under a second address. Both are legitimate events that must be reconciled, not dropped. | ✅  |
| **Callsign reuse, in-flight change and the same tail flying two flights a day** | A callsign identifies a flight, not an aircraft. The same callsign is used every day by whichever airframe operates that rotation, several different airframes may fly it over a week, and one airframe flies several callsigns in a day. Callsigns are also reused within a day on long-haul routes.                                                                                                     | ADS-B carries flight identification in the aircraft identification message (type codes 1-4) as eight characters drawn from a restricted six-bit character set — uppercase letters, digits and space — padded with trailing spaces. It is crew- or system-enterable, not derived from the airframe.                 | A regional operator's morning rotation and evening rotation carry different callsigns on the same airframe; the following day the same two callsigns are flown by a different aircraft from the same fleet. (Ordinary scheduled-operations pattern; illustrative rather than a cited schedule.)                                                                                            | Grouping telemetry history by callsign. Yesterday's flight and today's flight merge into one continuous track, producing a 'flight' that teleports between airports overnight and a total distance that is the sum of several days' flying. (verify)                                                                                                                                                        | The callsign is the most human-meaningful string in the message, so it becomes the display key and then, quietly, the grouping key. Grouping is where it fails: a callsign is unique within a flight, not across time, and nothing in the data marks where one flight ends and the next begins.                                                                            | Model a flight leg as its own entity with a start and end, bounded by air/ground transitions rather than by callsign changes, and attach the callsign to the leg. Never let a callsign span a ground period. (verify)                                                                                                       | RTCA DO-260B — aircraft identification message, type codes 1-4, 8-character flight identification field.                                       | Flag any flight leg whose start and end airports differ from the previous leg's end, or whose elapsed time exceeds the type's endurance. Both indicate legs have been merged.                      | ✅  |

| **Time of applicability vs time of receipt in a store-and-forward link** | In a satellite or store-and-forward link, the moment a position was measured and the moment the ground system receives it are two different times, separated by seconds to minutes. Both are real and they answer different questions: where was the aircraft, versus what do we know now. | Iridium SBD network transmit latency for a mobile-originated message ranges from about 5 seconds for a 70-byte message to roughly 20 seconds at maximum length, with observed send times of 6 to 22 seconds. The service is explicitly documented as unsuitable where very low latency is required.                         | A position measured at 18:32:00Z is transmitted over SBD and arrives at the ground application at 18:32:19Z. At 300 kt the aircraft has moved about 1.6 nm in the interval, and if the queue was backed up the same report may arrive after a newer one. (Latency figure is sourced; the derived displacement is arithmetic on it.) | Ordering by receipt time. Reports that were queued during a link outage arrive in a burst, out of order relative to their measurement times. A current-position projection updated in arrival order ends up showing an older position as the latest one, and the aircraft appears to jump backwards along its own track.                                       | Receipt time is the timestamp the ingest layer has closest to hand — it is `now()` — and it is always monotonic, which makes it look like a safe ordering key. It orders by when the network delivered the message, which is not the order in which the world happened.         | Carry both timestamps explicitly: time of applicability (when the position was valid) and time of receipt. Order and de-duplicate by time of applicability; guard the current-state projection so an older applicability time cannot overwrite a newer one; use receipt time only for link-health metrics and latency alerting. (verify) | Iridium Short Burst Data service description — latency characteristics and the explicit unsuitability for low-latency applications.                              | Histogram receipt_time minus applicability_time per aircraft and per link. The tail of that distribution is link health; a count of rejected out-of-order writes is the projection guard doing its job. | ✅  |
| **GPS time, leap seconds and week-number rollover**                      | GPS time is not UTC. It runs without leap seconds and counts weeks in a field that wraps. Converting between the two needs the current GPS-UTC offset, and receivers that mishandle the wrap report a date twenty years wrong while reporting a perfectly good position.                   | The legacy GPS week number is a 10-bit field with range 0-1023, so it rolls over every 1024 weeks — about 19.6 years. The second rollover occurred at GPS midnight on 6/7 April 2019, which was 23:59:42 UTC on 6 April 2019 because the GPS-UTC offset was 18 leap seconds. GPS time itself is unaffected by leap seconds. | A receiver whose firmware hard-codes a rollover window based on its build date reports the correct time of day and the correct position, with a date roughly 19.6 years in the past.                                                                                                                                                | A telemetry report with a valid position and a date from 2006. Ordering by timestamp puts it at the beginning of history; a retention policy deletes it; a 'reports in the last minute' query never sees it; and a natural key on (aircraft, timestamp) accepts it as a distinct observation rather than recognising it as today's report with a broken clock. | Timestamp validation usually checks that the string parses, not that the instant is plausible. A rolled-over date parses perfectly. The failure then surfaces far from its cause — as a gap in a chart, or a row that retention removed — which makes it expensive to diagnose. | Reject or quarantine timestamps outside a plausible window around receipt time rather than trusting the parse; the same windowing that receivers use to disambiguate the rollover works at the application boundary. Keep the raw value on the quarantined record so the cause is diagnosable.                                           | IS-GPS-200 — GPS interface specification, week number field; the CNAV message extends the week number to 13 bits, pushing the rollover period out to 8192 weeks. | A counter of reports rejected for an implausible timestamp, labelled by aircraft. A single airframe producing all of them is a receiver firmware problem, not a data problem.                           | ✅  |

| **Iridium Short Burst Data — message size, latency and cost per report**   | Satellite position reporting is metered by the byte and constrained by message size. That single fact explains most of the design of real fleet-tracking telemetry: why reports are small and binary rather than JSON, why the reporting interval is minutes rather than seconds, and why the interval changes with circumstances.      | Iridium 9601 and 9602 transceivers transmit a maximum of 340 bytes per mobile-originated SBD message. Network transmit latency ranges from about 5 seconds at 70 bytes to roughly 20 seconds at maximum length.                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                     | A position report packed into well under 340 bytes as a binary structure — latitude, longitude, altitude, groundspeed, track and a status word — rather than the roughly 300 bytes the same data occupies as formatted JSON. (The 340-byte ceiling is sourced; the JSON size comparison is an illustration.)                                                                                                    | Designing the wire format as verbose JSON and the reporting interval as 'every few seconds', then discovering that the airborne link cannot carry it and the airtime cost is prohibitive. The ground-side API and the satellite link have opposite constraints, and a system that assumes one format end to end has to be rebuilt at the boundary.                                                 | A ground-side developer's intuition is that bandwidth is free and JSON is the default. Over SBD, every byte is billed and the message ceiling is 340 bytes, so the airborne format is a packed binary structure and the readable JSON only exists after a gateway has expanded it. Skipping that distinction produces a design that cannot fly. | Treat the airborne wire format and the ground API schema as two different formats with an explicit translation layer, and size the reporting interval from the airtime budget rather than from what the dashboard would like. Keep messages well under the ceiling: latency scales with length.                    | Iridium Short Burst Data Service Best Practice Guide, Release 2.0 — message sizing, latency and duty-cycle guidance. | Bytes per aircraft per hour, and the resulting airtime cost, as a first-class operational metric. A reporting-rate change that looks free on the dashboard is a line item on the bill.                                  | ✅  |
| **ACARS / ARINC 618 message structure and character-set limits**           | ACARS is the long-established character-oriented air/ground messaging system that carries position reports, engine data and free text. It is not a general-purpose transport: it segments messages into small blocks, uses a restricted character set, and identifies message types by a two-character label.                           | ARINC 618 segments ACARS messages into blocks of 220 usable characters, with each block's text limited to 220 characters. Transmission is character-oriented using 7-bit ASCII with an odd parity bit as the eighth bit; although the system character set is ASCII, ARINC 618 notes most of the network supports only a reduced ASCII set. A new block is not sent until the previous block is acknowledged. Message types are identified by two-character labels. | A downlink position report carried as a labelled ACARS block of at most 220 characters, in uppercase restricted ASCII, acknowledged block by block before the next is sent. (Composed from the sourced block size, charset and acknowledgement behaviour; not a captured message.)                                                                                                                              | Assuming the field is UTF-8 and arbitrary length. Any non-ASCII character in an operator name or a free-text remark cannot be represented, and a message longer than 220 characters is silently segmented — so a consumer that reads one block gets a truncated record that still parses.                                                                                                          | Modern text handling assumes Unicode and unbounded strings, so a schema declares `text` and moves on. The link cannot carry that, and the failure is not an error — it is a shortened or transliterated message that looks valid on arrival.                                                                                                    | Constrain the field at the schema boundary to the restricted character set and the block length, reassemble multi-block messages before parsing, and reject rather than truncate. Do not accept characters the downlink could never have produced. (verify)                                                        | ARINC 618-8 (2016) — Air/Ground Character-Oriented Protocol Specification.                                           | Count messages rejected for out-of-charset characters and multi-block messages reassembled versus received. A rise in single-block records for a message type that is normally multi-block means reassembly is failing. | ✅  |
| **What triggers a position report — periodic, event, and rate escalation** | A position report is not a metronome. Real tracking systems report on a slow periodic cadence in normal flight, and escalate to a much faster rate on an event — a distress condition, a deviation, a crew action. The interval is a variable of the situation, not a constant of the system.                                           | The escalation floor is regulatory rather than protocol-level: an aeroplane in a distress condition must autonomously transmit information from which a position can be determined at least once every minute, and the transmitting system must activate autonomously on detection of the condition and be self-powered if the aircraft electrical system fails.                                                                                                    | Normal cruise reporting on a multi-minute interval to control airtime cost, escalating to one-minute reporting when a distress condition is detected.                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                           | A staleness rule with one fixed threshold. If 'no report for two minutes means stale' is tuned for the distress rate, every normally-operating aircraft on a slow cadence is permanently stale; if it is tuned for the cruise rate, an aircraft that has stopped reporting during an escalation goes unnoticed for far too long. (verify)                                                          | The naive staleness check compares elapsed time against a constant. The correct comparison is against that aircraft's currently expected interval, which the system must therefore know and track. A constant cannot express a variable.                                                                                                        | Store the expected reporting interval per aircraft as state, derive staleness as a multiple of it rather than as an absolute duration, and treat a change in reporting rate as itself an event worth surfacing — an aircraft that starts reporting every minute has, by definition, had something happen. (verify) | ICAO Annex 6 Part I — location of an aeroplane in distress; GADSS concept of operations.                             | Report the observed inter-arrival interval per aircraft alongside the expected one. ## Bubble-up — things worth a look

- 💡 The GADSS one-minute distress-tracking date has moved twice and most secondary sources still print the original 2021 figure. — _Anything quoting 2021 is repeating superseded trade-press coverage. As adopted the requirement applies from 1 January 2025 to aeroplanes over 27,000 kg whose certificate of airworthiness was first issued on or after 1 January 2024. This is a live example of a regulatory citation that decays, and an argument for recording a retrieval date on every regulatory claim._ ([src](https://aviationweek.com/mro/icao-postpones-distress-tracking-mandate))
- 💡 Gas generator speed is reported as a percentage of a reference speed, and 100% is about 37,500 rpm on a PT6A-27. — _This is the single highest-value finding of the run for open-aviation-telemetry. The existing schema has engine.rpm with a 0-6000 range and an 1800-2700 airborne band. For a turbine that is not a units error — the modelled quantity does not exist, the real shaft speed is an order of magnitude outside the declared range, and the operationally limiting parameter (ITT) has no field. It is a schema change, not a constant change._ ([src](https://www.aviationpros.com/engines-components/article/10387616/troubleshooting-taking-a-look-at-the-pt6a27-engine))
- 💡 Fleet-wide NACp degradation is a GNSS interference detector, not a fleet of broken transponders. — _A tracking platform that stores the integrity categories gets regional GNSS interference monitoring for free — a genuinely useful product feature that falls out of not discarding fields. A platform that stores only latitude and longitude cannot ever add it retrospectively, because the data was never kept._ ([src](https://insidegnss.com/protecting-gnss-for-safe-aviation/))
- 💡 SBD latency scales with message length: about 5 s at 70 bytes versus about 20 s at the 340-byte maximum. — _Message size is not only a cost lever, it is a latency lever. That inverts the usual instinct to batch: for a tracking application, several small reports arrive sooner than one large one, which matters directly for how quickly a position is known._ ([src](https://metocean.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/06/DEV_Iridium-SBD_Best-Practices-Guide_NOV17.pdf))
- 🔎 gap Specific ARINC 429 label numbers for magnetic versus true heading were not obtainable from public sources. — _ARINC 429 Part 1 is paywalled and public summaries document the word structure without reproducing the full label table. The structural claim — that units, resolution and range are fixed per label, so magnetic and true heading are distinct labels — is sourced; the specific label numbers are not and were therefore not asserted._ ([src](https://www.aim-online.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/07/aim-tutorial-oview429-190712-u.pdf))
- 🔎 gap The DO-260B NIC/NACp/SIL value tables themselves were not retrievable; only their existence and location were confirmed. — _RTCA DO-260B is a paid standard. Public sources confirm the tables are in §2.2.3 and describe what each category means, but do not reproduce the integer-to-containment-radius mapping. Any implementation needs the document; quoting specific radii from memory would be exactly the confabulation this rubric's source-required rule exists to prevent._ ([src](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2023/10/17/2023-22710/inclusion-of-additional-automatic-dependent-surveillance-broadcast-ads-b-out-technical-standard))
- 🧩 spec Add a field for the cost of getting this wrong, distinct from severity. — _Several subjects here are cheap to fix now and very expensive after data has accumulated — the turbine parameter set, storing both altitudes, storing integrity categories. Severity captures what breaks; it does not capture that the fix window closes. A `fix_window` field (before-schema / before-launch / anytime) would let a reader sort by urgency rather than by damage._
- 🧩 spec Several difficult cases are schema-shaped: the model cannot represent the truth, rather than computing it wrongly. — _Altitude (two quantities, one field), heading (value without reference), engine parameters (wrong quantity entirely), identifiers (four ids, one key), timestamps (two times, one field). That recurrence is itself the finding: the dominant failure mode in telemetry modelling is not bad logic, it is a schema that cannot express the domain. Worth stating explicitly in the rendered output._
